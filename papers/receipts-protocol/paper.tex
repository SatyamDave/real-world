\begin{abstract}
Claims that a forecasting system, in particular one built on large language models (LLMs), predicts real events well are hard to trust. Backtests leak outcomes through training data and retrieval, records can be written after the fact, misses can be dropped, the best of many variants or runs can be reported as the only one, grading can be softened, and the comparison baseline can be chosen after the outcome. We specify version~1 of \emph{Receipts}, an evaluation protocol for a forecaster that publishes its own record. Questions are selected from venue records by a rule that uses only attributes a verifier can reconstruct, with a public randomness beacon as the only source of randomness, and are committed before any forecast. Every forecast of every registered forecaster is a salted leaf in a daily Merkle tree whose root is hash-chained to the previous day and signed with a key fixed at genesis. Each day record must be shown, by RFC~3161 time-stamp tokens from a majority of three named authorities and an OpenTimestamps Bitcoin attestation, to have existed by a call time $\Ts$ fixed in advance, and must appear in a public transparency log by then. Each forecast is scored as a paired Brier (and log) difference against a manipulation-resistant market price at $\Ts$ that a verifier can recompute from venue history. Any cell that is unrevealed, late, unanchored or abstained scores the maximum loss. We model the operator's actions after questions are committed and show that each action we identified is impossible, charged to the forecaster, publicly logged or detectable, under stated assumptions: an honest clock, authentic venue records, and either independent witnesses or a monitored public log. Two residual channels remain: running a stochastic forecaster many times and committing the most market-like run, which we can detect only statistically, and Sybil records. The protocol proves integrity and time order, not accuracy. It is being built for Real World, a planned population simulation; no forecasts have been scored, and all empirical sections are pre-registered plans with pending results.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Forecasting is one of the few tasks where an AI system's competence can be checked against reality rather than against a static answer key. That advantage is easy to waste. Reported results for LLM forecasters mix retrospective questions, whose outcomes may sit in a model's training data or in documents a retrieval system returns, with live questions; \citet{paleka2025pitfalls} catalogue forms of temporal leakage that make many reported results hard to trust. Benchmarks that ask only about unresolved events, such as ForecastBench \citep{karger2024forecastbench}, Daily Oracle \citep{dai2025prescient} and Prophet Arena \citep{yang2025prophet}, remove outcome leakage by construction, but they are run by a benchmark operator on behalf of model makers. A company publishing its \emph{own} track record faces a sharper credibility problem: it controls the questions, the forecasts, the grading, the baseline, the timing and the record itself.

Real World, the system this protocol is being built for, is planned as a population of about $10^5$ synthetic agents, held as numeric records that language models seed and read, whose segment beliefs are corrected each morning against polls (the companion \emph{Don't Ask a Model, Ask a World}, in preparation, describes it). It is intended to forecast resolvable events daily and to commit those forecasts before their outcomes exist. None of this is running yet. This paper specifies how such forecasts should be recorded and scored so that a skeptical outsider can verify the record without trusting the operator. It is a methods paper and reports no forecasting results; everything in Section~\ref{sec:plan} is a pre-registered plan.

The design rests on one principle: \emph{any step whose integrity cannot be checked counts against the forecaster}. A missing reveal, a missing anchor, an abstention, a snapshot that disagrees with the venue, or a record that cannot be shown to precede its scheduled call time each score as the worst possible forecast. No timing or randomness is left to the operator: the call time is fixed by a public schedule and every random choice is derived from a public beacon.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A threat model for self-published forecast records with nineteen concrete manipulations, from backfilling and salt grinding to marking the close and hidden best-of-$k$ runs, each mapped to the protocol's response and labelled as prevented, charged to the forecaster, detectable, logged, or not prevented (Section~\ref{sec:threats}, Table~\ref{tab:threats}).
  \item The Receipts protocol, version~1: question selection from reconstructible venue attributes with a beacon seed, two-phase commitment (questions, then forecasts), positional salted Merkle leaves that bind the full forecaster-by-question grid, a signed hash-chained daily record, a public schedule that fixes the call time, a majority of three RFC~3161 authorities plus a Bitcoin attestation, and entry in a public transparency log as a de facto witness until dedicated witnesses exist (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}).
  \item A scoring rule: paired Brier and log-score differences against a windowed median of the venue's bid-ask midpoint at the scheduled call time, with spread and volume floors, a maximum-loss treatment of every uncheckable or abstained cell, an explicit unit of analysis, a wild cluster bootstrap-t over venue-defined events, an equivalence margin, and a stated power analysis (Section~\ref{sec:scoring}).
  \item An explicit model of the operator's actions after question commitment, two propositions with proof sketches on time order and on the scored set, and a table of how each modelled action is disposed of (Section~\ref{sec:guarantees}, Table~\ref{tab:choices}). The propositions cover the actions we modelled; they are not a proof that no other action exists.
  \item A pre-registered evaluation plan with six hypotheses, including a three-arm measurement of retrieval leakage for an LLM-only forecaster and a rerun audit that tests whether committed forecasts were selected toward the market (Section~\ref{sec:plan}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Scoring probabilistic forecasts.} The Brier score \citep{brier1950} and the logarithmic score are strictly proper \citep{gneiting2007proper}: reporting one's true belief uniquely maximises expected score, which is what makes a public score meaningful. \citet{murphy1973} decomposed the Brier score into reliability, resolution and uncertainty, and \citet{ferro2012bias} show that the plug-in decomposition is biased and give a correction. \citet{gneiting2007calibration} frame evaluation as maximising sharpness subject to calibration; \citet{arnold2024crps} decompose mean CRPS; \citet{ferro2014fair} gives fair scores for ensembles with exchangeable members. For comparing two forecasters on the same targets, \citet{diebold1995} test equal expected loss, \citet{diebold2015} clarifies that the test compares forecasts rather than models, and \citet{giacomini2006} test conditional predictive ability. For dependent observations we use cluster-robust inference \citep{cameron2011multiway} and, with few clusters, the wild cluster bootstrap-t \citep{cameron2008bootstrap}; for a claim of practical equality we use two one-sided tests \citep{schuirmann1987}. We use these tools unchanged; our contribution is the record they are applied to.

\paragraph{Forecasting competitions and LLM forecasting.} Forecasting tournaments \citep{mellers2014} and the M competitions \citep{makridakis2020m4} established out-of-sample evaluation with a fixed question set; M6 ran live, with forecasts submitted over time \citep{makridakis2025m6}. For LLMs, \citet{schoenegger2023} entered GPT-4 in a live Metaculus tournament; \citet{schoenegger2024silicon} found an ensemble of twelve LLMs statistically indistinguishable from a human crowd on 31 questions, which motivates the multi-sample design of our LLM-only baseline; \citet{halawi2024} evaluate a retrieval system on questions published after the models' knowledge cut-offs; Autocast pairs questions with a date-organised news corpus \citep{zou2022autocast}; ForecastBench asks only about events with no known answer at submission time \citep{karger2024forecastbench}; and Daily Oracle generates questions from each day's news and evaluates models continuously and forward in time \citep{dai2025prescient}, the closest precedent for our forward-only framing. Two recent results bear on our choice of baseline. Against a \emph{market}, Prophet Arena reports, over 1{,}367 resolved Kalshi events (76\% of them sports, each event weighted equally), a Brier score of 0.184 for the best of 23 evaluated LLM configurations against 0.187 for the market, each with a bootstrapped 95\% interval of $\pm 0.006$; the intervals overlap, the best-of-23 selection is itself the selection our threat model calls M4, and the market lags several LLMs when forecasts are made far in advance \citep{yang2025prophet}. The fair summary is that the best LLMs roughly match the market on that question mix. Against \emph{people}, Metaculus's FutureEval reports a head-to-head score of $-1.25$ for its bot team against professional forecasters over 99 questions, where the head-to-head score is a peer score between two participants and negative values favour the professionals; its 95\% interval includes zero \citep{metaculus2026futureeval}. On markets themselves, \citet{atanasov2017} found market prices more accurate than the simple mean of prediction polls, but statistically aggregated team polls more accurate than the market, a reminder that a market is a strong but beatable baseline. Neither design lets an outsider verify a record the forecaster publishes about itself. Public track records such as those of Metaculus and the Good Judgment tournaments \citep{mellers2014} are kept by a platform on behalf of forecasters, not by the forecaster.

\paragraph{Contamination.} Benchmark contamination in LLMs has been argued to be severe and is largely unmeasured \citep{sainz2023contamination}; it can be detected for some datasets by guided completion \citep{golchin2023timetravel}; benchmarks refreshed from recent sources limit it \citep{white2024livebench}. Effective knowledge cut-offs often differ from the cut-offs model makers report \citep{cheng2024dated}, and LLMs recall exact economic values from before their cut-off even when told not to \citep{lopezlira2025memorization}. In finance, \citet{glasserman2023lookahead} assess look-ahead bias in LLM sentiment-based return predictions by anonymising headlines, and \citet{he2025chrono} train models only on text available at each date, which removes the training-data side of look-ahead bias by construction. These are the closest prior measurements to our hypothesis H4, which targets the retrieval side. For forecasting, \citet{paleka2025pitfalls} argue that temporal leakage takes many forms beyond training data. Forward-only evaluation avoids outcome leakage without auditing the model, because the outcome does not exist when the forecast is fixed; Receipts makes forward-only a checkable property rather than a promise.

\paragraph{Selective reporting.} The forking paths of \citet{gelman2014crisis} and pre-registration in the sense of \citet{nosek2018} are the scientific framing. \citet{singh2025leaderboard} document the leaderboard version: providers privately testing many variants on Chatbot Arena and publishing the best, which is manipulation M4 below.

\paragraph{Commitments, timestamping, randomness and transparency logs.} Hash commitments are binding under collision resistance; hiding does not follow from collision resistance alone, and \citet{halevi1996commit} give a construction from collision-free hashing with provable hiding. \citet{haber1991} introduced linked timestamping; \citet{merkle1988} trees let one root commit to many leaves; \citet{schneier1999audit} give tamper-evident audit logs; \citet{crosby2009} give history trees with logarithmic proofs. Certificate Transparency standardises inclusion and consistency proofs and domain-separates leaf and node hashes \citep{rfc6962,rfc9162}; client gossip lets verifiers detect a log that shows them inconsistent views \citep{chuat2015gossip}. Sigstore's Rekor applies a public transparency log to software signing and records, for each entry, the time it was added \citep{newman2022sigstore,rekor_openapi}. Witness cosigning \citep{syta2016witness}, now specified for transparency logs by C2SP \citep{c2sp_witness}, has independent parties cosign a log head only if it extends the one they last saw, which blocks split views. RFC~3161 time-stamp authorities sign a hash and a time \citep{rfc3161}. Bitcoin \citep{nakamoto2008} was used for decentralised document timestamping by \citet{gipp2015}; OpenTimestamps aggregates digests into Bitcoin transactions so that trust rests on the blockchain rather than on the calendar servers, and its author notes that a timestamp proves only that \emph{a} message existed before some time, not that it is \emph{the} message \citep{todd2016ots}, which is the root of the parallel-chain problem in Section~\ref{sec:threats}. Bitcoin block timestamps can be set by miners with little risk of detection \citep{szalachowski2018timestamps}, which is why our Bitcoin bound rests on an honest-majority assumption. Public randomness beacons publish unpredictable, verifiable values at fixed times: NIST specifies a beacon format and protocol \citep{nist8213}, and drand is a distributed beacon run by a consortium \citep{drand}. We use a beacon so that no random choice in the protocol is under the operator's control.

\paragraph{Sealed forecasts in science.} The Financial Bubble Experiment sealed forecasts with digital authentication and delayed disclosure \citep{sornette2010fbe}; the Axial Seamount experiment applies the same design to volcanic eruptions \citep{lei2025axial}. \citet{irving2016blockchain} proposed timestamping clinical-trial protocols in the Bitcoin blockchain; the article was retracted at the authors' request after methodological concerns raised in post-publication peer review \citep{irving2017retraction}, and one referee's report found that nothing in the recorded transaction linked it to the protocol's hash \citep{knottenbelt2017report}, a reminder that anchoring code needs independent verification (our H2). \citet{jyotish2026sealed} (published, awaiting peer review) anchors a manifest of hashed forecasts with OpenTimestamps, freezes a grading rubric at seal time, states falsification conditions in advance, and is explicit that the anchor proves integrity, not anteriority, for events that resolved before anchoring. Receipts differs in four ways: the questions are chosen by a mechanical rule from venue records rather than by the operator; the selection rule and scoring rules are frozen before any forecast rather than with each forecast; excluded, abstained or unrevealed material is charged at the maximum loss rather than published with reasons; and the baseline is the market at a scheduled time rather than a base rate.

\section{Threat model}
\label{sec:threats}

The adversary is the operator of the record. It runs the forecasters, writes the selection rule, holds the salts and the signing key, controls the git mirror, fetches venue data, and publishes the scores. It wants the published record to look better than its forecasts were. It cannot find SHA-256 collisions or second preimages, cannot forge signatures, cannot rewrite Bitcoin's history, and cannot predict or bias the public beacon. Readers, including competitors and journalists, are verifiers who can run open-source code against public data. Third parties may also try to move the baseline (M17).

We state the assumptions on which defences depend. \emph{Clock}: either at least two of the three time-stamp authorities (TSAs) named at genesis are honest, or Bitcoin miners are an honest majority in the sense of Section~\ref{sec:protocol}. \emph{Authentic venue records}: the venues' APIs report their own listings, contract attributes, quotes and settlements truthfully to every caller; the protocol does not authenticate venue responses, which carry no venue signature. \emph{Single public chain}: only one record per day can be presented. In \emph{witnessed mode}, independent witnesses cosign each record head \citep{syta2016witness,c2sp_witness}. In \emph{logged mode}, which is how the protocol will launch, each day record must appear in Rekor's public log under the genesis key by its call time; the assumption then holds for any verifier who scans the log for entries under that key, provided the log itself is append-only and does not show different views to different readers, which its consistency proofs and gossip \citep{chuat2015gossip} let readers check. We label every aggregate with its mode. We do not defend against insider knowledge of outcomes that the market also lacks (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

Table~\ref{tab:threats} lists the manipulations. Contamination is the honest-operator version of M1: no intent is needed for a backtest to know the answer. M4, M5, M14 and M18 are forking-path problems \citep{gelman2014crisis}: each run may be honest, but the selection over runs is not. M14 is the strongest attack on any timestamp-only design: from one public genesis the operator can grow several chains privately, anchor all of them, and publish the best after outcomes are known. RFC~3161 tokens and Bitcoin attestations do not stop it, because each proves that a hash existed, not that it was the only one or that anyone saw it. Its value grows slowly with the number of chains: if $k$ chains had independent mean paired differences with standard deviation $\sigma_\Delta/\sqrt{n}$, the best of them would improve the mean by roughly $\sigma_\Delta\sqrt{2\ln k / n}$; for illustration, with $\sigma_\Delta = 0.03$, $n = 100$ and $k = 10$ that is about $0.006$, twice the market gap Prophet Arena reports \citep{yang2025prophet}. A handful of chains is therefore enough, which is why logged mode is required from the first day.

\section{The Receipts protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

\subsection{Objects and registered forecasters}

A \emph{question} record contains an identifier, the question text, the outcome space (binary, categorical with an ordered or unordered list, or numeric with the venue's brackets), the resolution source, the resolution rule including tie-breaks and a fallback source, the latest resolution time $\tau_q$, a void rule, a cluster identifier, a category, the reveal mode (clear or embargoed, P6), and the venue and contract identifier of the matching prediction market, if any. The cluster identifier, category and reveal mode are not chosen per question: the cluster is the venue's event identifier (Kalshi's \texttt{event\_ticker} \citep{kalshi_market}; the equivalent field on other venues, named in the genesis record), the category is read from a lookup table committed at genesis that maps each venue series to one category, and the reveal mode is a function of the category fixed by the genesis reveal policy. When a market exists, the resolution rule and void rule \emph{are} the venue's settlement and cancellation, so the operator does not grade market questions. Each Kalshi market is a binary question; markets of one multi-market event (for example the brackets of one release) share a cluster and are never treated as independent.

A \emph{forecaster} is a named registry slot whose content is a versioned pipeline. Each version is identified by $h_{\mathrm{cfg}}$, the hash of its code, prompts and configuration, together with the provider snapshot identifiers of every model it calls (an API model can change under an unchanged configuration hash, so the snapshot identifier is recorded separately). For each cell the pipeline also produces an \emph{input manifest} whose hash $h_{\mathrm{in}}$ enters the leaf: hashes of every input document and data snapshot read, the random seeds used, the hashes of the news items dropped by the market filter, and the hash of the log of every model call with its output. The registry $F$ planned for the first window is:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{rw-blind} (confirmatory slot): the full Real World simulation with every prediction-market source removed from its inputs and from its belief assimilation, and with news items dropped if they match a keyword filter (venue names, ``odds'', ``betting'', ``prediction market'' and similar terms) whose exact list is committed at genesis. Its random seeds are derived from the day's beacon value (P1), and its forecast is the pre-registered aggregate of its ensemble members, whose values are in the call log. The operator runs a pipeline test that fails if a market source is read; that test is run by the operator and is trusted, not verified.
  \item \texttt{rw-aware}: the market-aware configuration, the same simulation with market prices among its inputs, last read at a scheduled time $\ta$.
  \item \texttt{llm-only}: a same-feed LLM, one LLM given the same news context as the simulation and no population, five samples, trimmed mean.
  \item Further slots that companion papers pre-register (for example the matched same-feed baseline of \emph{Don't Ask a Model, Ask a World}), each committed in the registry at genesis.
  \item \texttt{copy-tc} and \texttt{copy-ta}: the market price at $\tc$ and at $\ta$ under the rule of P5. These are controls: they show what copying a lagged market price scores against the market at $\Ts$.
  \item Naive baselines: $0.5$, and the base rate of the outcome among the record's questions in the same category resolved before $\tQ$ (0.5 until that category has a committed minimum count).
\end{itemize}
The market at $\Ts$ is the comparison baseline, not a registry entry. A slot keeps every version it ever had: a new version applies only to questions committed after it, no version can be retired from the slot's record, and the headline for a slot pools all its versions. From the start of window $W_1$ until its analysis date, \texttt{rw-blind} may change version only when a provider retires a pinned model snapshot; such a forced change is logged with the provider's notice.

All records are serialised with the JSON Canonicalization Scheme \citep{rfc8785}. Probabilities are integers in units of $10^{-4}$, never floats, so that canonical bytes do not depend on number formatting.

\subsection{The daily schedule}

The genesis record fixes a daily schedule in UTC: the last market read of \texttt{rw-aware} at $\ta(d)$, question commitment at $\tQ(d)$, the forecasters' data cut-off at $\tc(d)$, and the call time $\Ts(d) = \tQ(d) + \delta$, with $\ta \le \tQ \le \tc < \Ts$. These are calendar constants, not operator observations, so no later act of the operator can change which market price a forecast is compared against. The cut-off $\tc$ is a commitment by the operator that the protocol cannot enforce; the information time a verifier can bound is $\Ts$ under the TSA assumption, or the Bitcoin bound $t_{\mathrm{BTC}} \le \Ts + \Gamma$ without it. Table~\ref{tab:constants} lists the planned values.

The lead margin excludes most same-day and next-day questions: with $\lambda = 24$~h, a question must resolve at least a day after the call time. This is a deliberate cost of not trusting a single clock.

\subsection{Phases}

\paragraph{P0: Genesis.} Before the first forecast, the operator publishes a genesis record containing the protocol version (this paper specifies version~1), the public signing key that must sign every later record, the hash of the reference verifier, the registry and the market keyword filter, the selection rule with its venue fields, series list, category table and excluded categories (Section~\ref{sec:ethics}), the scoring and analysis plan of Sections~\ref{sec:scoring} and~\ref{sec:plan} including the window dates, the constants of Table~\ref{tab:constants}, the three TSAs' identities and certificates (operated by unrelated organisations; a free single-operator service may be one of them but never the only one), the OpenTimestamps calendars, the beacon (drand's main chain \citep{drand}) and the rule mapping each day to a beacon round, the transparency log (Rekor's public instance), the witness set (empty at launch), the reveal-mode policy, the name of the independent implementer of H2 and the hash of the specification version given to them, a list of any backtests run while designing the selection rule, venue list or category table, and a minimum public reporting horizon (the record runs and is reported at least until window $W_1$ is analysed). The genesis hash is announced in several public places and entered in the transparency log. Changes take effect only through a later signed record and apply only to questions committed after it. From the start of $W_1$ until its analysis date, the selection rule, the constants, the definition of \texttt{rw-blind} (except forced snapshot changes), the keyword filter, the cluster and category rules and the analysis plan are frozen; a change record dated inside that interval is a protocol violation and is reported as one.

\paragraph{Pre-genesis records.} An earlier internal design (the company's decision record for a launch ledger) is implemented in the company's engine (\nolinkurl{engine/rw/ledger.py} and \nolinkurl{engine/rw/score.py}, version 0.1.0). It produces daily hashes before this protocol's anchors and witnesses exist, with a different format: leaves sorted by hash rather than by position, no records for empty days, correction entries that supersede earlier leaves, a market price stored inside the leaf, questions from a fixed list rather than beacon selection, no signature and no transparency-log entry. Its anchors are one RFC~3161 token from a single free authority, whose imprint and time the engine read but whose signature and certificate chain it does not check, and an OpenTimestamps calendar proof that stays pending, because the engine does not yet upgrade it to a Bitcoin attestation; its skill intervals use a day-block bootstrap. Under this paper's rules each of those differences reopens a manipulation (M3, M2, M7, M8). The genesis record therefore names that ledger and commits the hash of its last day record, so it cannot later be presented as a second chain, and declares that no cell from it enters any Receipts claim or hypothesis. Version~1 of this specification supersedes that format for every record that claims Receipts properties. At repository commit \texttt{bff759c} the daily cycle requests both anchors right after it commits a day, for days on the live question lists only (days on the illustrative fixture questions are never sent); a day counts as anchored once a stored token's imprint equals its record hash, and a failed or still-pending request is recorded and retried on the next run without stopping scoring. No live day has been anchored yet, because no live question list is approved. The same cycle writes an export of each day (the day record, its hash, and every entry with its salt and leaf hash, but no anchor proofs), so a published day reveals its values when it is committed, not after a call time. A separate command pushes these exports to a public git repository without force; it refuses to change a day already mirrored, skips fixture days, and stays off until the operator names the repository. As in P4, that mirror carries no trust.

\paragraph{P1: Question commitment at $\tQ$.} The beacon round for day $d$ is the first round scheduled at or after $\tQ(d)$; its value $\beta_d$ is public, verifiable and unknown to anyone before that time \citep{drand,nist8213}. Shortly after $\tQ(d)$ the operator fetches the named venues' records and publishes them raw. The candidate universe $U_d$ is defined only by attributes that a verifier can read from venue records later: a contract is in $U_d$ if its venue record shows creation and opening at or before $\tQ(d)$ and closing after it (Kalshi's \texttt{created\_time}, \texttt{open\_time} and \texttt{close\_time} \citep{kalshi_market}), its series is in the committed list, its category is not excluded, and it is eligible: $\Ts(d) + \lambda \le \tau_q \le \Ts(d) + H_{\max}$, with $\tau_q$ the venue's latest possible expiration time (Kalshi's \texttt{latest\_expiration\_time}). No criterion may use a price, a quote, volume, open interest or any other attribute that changes after $\tQ$; such a criterion would let the operator choose among responses fetched at different moments and could not be rebuilt. Because $U_d$ is a function of the venue's records, not of the response the operator happened to keep, the time of the fetch within the slot does not matter, and an omitted contract is detectable by anyone who queries the venue later. A venue may amend a contract's attributes after $\tQ$; a verifier treats a contract whose venue record was updated after $\tQ(d)$ as unverifiable rather than as an omission, which leaves a small residual channel that we report (H2). Of the contracts in $U_d$ not yet in the record, the first $k_{\mathrm{new}}$ in the order of $\Hh(\beta_d \,\|\, \text{venue} \,\|\, \text{contract id})$ are selected; this is $Q_d$. A contract enters the record once, on the day it is selected, and is forecast on that day and on every later day while $\tau_q \ge \Ts(d) + \lambda$. The operator publishes $U_d$, $Q_d$ and $\beta_d$, signed and stamped by the TSAs. The seed of every random draw a forecaster makes on day $d$ is $\Hh(\beta_d \,\|\, f \,\|\, q \,\|\, \text{draw label})$.

\paragraph{P2: Forecasting.} Every forecaster $f \in F$ produces $p_{f,q}$ for every question open in the record on day $d$ from information up to $\tc(d)$. A forecaster that cannot produce a forecast emits an explicit abstention.

\paragraph{P3: Commitment.} For each cell the operator draws a 32-byte salt $s_{f,q,d}$ from a cryptographic random source and forms the leaf
\begin{equation}
  \ell_{f,q,d} = \Hh\bigl(\texttt{0x00} \,\|\, \canon(f, q, d, p_{f,q,d}, h_{\mathrm{cfg}}, \text{model snapshot ids}, h_{\mathrm{in}}) \,\|\, s_{f,q,d}\bigr),
\end{equation}
with an abstention encoded as a distinguished value of $p$. Salts no longer influence any choice in the protocol, so grinding them gains nothing. Leaves are placed in lexicographic order of $(f,q)$; inner nodes use the prefix \texttt{0x01}, following \citet{rfc6962}; the root of an empty tree is the hash of the empty string, as in RFC~6962. The day record is
\begin{equation}
  R_d = \canon\bigl(d,\ \mathrm{root}_d,\ |F|\cdot|O_d|,\ \Hh(O_d),\ \Hh(Q_d),\ \Hh(U_d),\ \beta_d,\ \Hh(F),\ \Hh(\mathcal{A}_{d-1}),\ \Hh(S_{d-1}),\ \Hh(R_{d-1})\bigr),
\end{equation}
signed with the genesis key, where $O_d$ is the list of questions open in the record on day $d$, $\mathcal{A}_{d-1}$ is the set of every anchor proof obtained for $R_{d-1}$ (all TSA tokens, all OpenTimestamps receipts and the log entry, not a chosen subset), and $S_{d-1}$ is the previous day's snapshot record (P5). The leaf count and hashes fix which cells must exist and the positional order fixes where each sits, so there is no room for an alternative leaf. A day with no open questions publishes $R_d$ with an empty tree and count zero. There are no correction entries: a committed cell is never superseded.

\paragraph{P4: Anchoring and publication by $\Ts$.} Before $\Ts(d)$ the operator (i) publishes $R_d$ and its signature to its public git mirror and submits it to the Wayback Machine and Software Heritage, which are weak extra witnesses with server-side capture times; (ii) obtains an RFC~3161 token from each of the three named TSAs, each carrying the hash, a serial number and a generation time \citep{rfc3161}; (iii) submits $\Hh(R_d)$ to the named OpenTimestamps calendars, whose pending receipts are later upgraded to a Bitcoin attestation \citep{todd2016ots}; (iv) enters the signed $\Hh(R_d)$ in Rekor's public log, whose entry records the time it was added \citep{rekor_openapi}; and (v) in witnessed mode, obtains timestamped cosignatures of the record chain's head from the witness set \citep{c2sp_witness}. The git mirror carries no trust: its dates are set by the committer, and the verifier ignores them. The Bitcoin bound is
\begin{equation}
  t_{\mathrm{BTC}} = \max\{\text{timestamp of blocks } B, B+1, \dots, B+k-1\} + \Delta_B,
\end{equation}
where $B$ is the block containing the attestation. A block timestamp must exceed the median of the previous eleven blocks and be at most two hours ahead of the node's network-adjusted time \citep{bitcoincore_chain,bip113}, but these rules alone do not bound how far a timestamp can lag the true time, and miners can set timestamps with little risk of detection \citep{szalachowski2018timestamps}. The bound is therefore valid only under an honest-majority assumption: at least one of $k$ consecutive blocks carries a timestamp set by an honest miner within $\Delta_B$ of true time. The day record is \emph{timely} if at least two committed TSA tokens have generation time at most $\Ts(d)$, the Bitcoin bound satisfies $t_{\mathrm{BTC}} \le \Ts(d) + \Gamma$, the log entry was added at most at $\Ts(d)$, and, in witnessed mode, a witness cosignature is timestamped at most $\Ts(d)$. Every cell of a day record that is not timely, or whose anchor proofs are missing from $\mathcal{A}_d$ in $R_{d+1}$, scores the maximum loss. Obtaining extra tokens gains nothing, because no proof moves $\Ts$. Two distinct day records for one day signed with the genesis key and found in the log are equivocation; a verifier who finds them marks the whole chain as failed from that day. The log's search index is best-effort \citep{rekor_openapi}, so verification scans the log rather than relying on search.

\paragraph{P5: Market snapshot.} The baseline is a quantity a verifier can recompute from venue history. For Kalshi, whose one-minute candlesticks report open, high, low and close for yes bid, yes ask and trade price, plus volume, and label each candle by \texttt{end\_period\_ts}, the inclusive end of its period \citep{kalshi_candlesticks}, take the $w$ one-minute candles whose \texttt{end\_period\_ts} lies in $(\Ts(d) - w\text{ min}, \Ts(d)]$. A candle is \emph{usable} if its closing yes bid $b$ and closing yes ask $a$ satisfy $0 < b < a < 1$; its mid is $(a+b)/2$ and its spread $a-b$. The baseline $m_q(\Ts)$ is the median mid over usable candles. The question has \emph{no market baseline} if fewer than $n_w$ candles are usable, the median spread exceeds $s_{\max}$, or the volume traded in the 24 hours ending at $\Ts$ (summed from hourly candles) is below $v_{\min}$. The same rule at $\tc$, $\ta$ and other times defines the copy controls and the sensitivity baselines. Kalshi serves candlesticks for settled markets from separate historical endpoints \citep{kalshi_historical}. Polymarket's price history returns one ``observed price'' per bucket without saying whether it is a mid or a trade, and keeps one-minute buckets for at least seven days and three- and twelve-hour buckets permanently \citep{polymarket_prices}; Polymarket questions are therefore scored descriptively, against the observed price of the one-minute bucket ending at $\Ts$, with the order book at $\Ts$ recorded in $S_d$, and are excluded from H6. Within $\delta_S$ after $\Ts(d)$ the operator fetches these history responses and order books and publishes them untouched, signed and stamped, as the snapshot record $S_d$, whose hash then enters $R_{d+1}$. The official baseline is always computed from $S_d$. A verifier recomputes it from venue history while the venue serves that history; if $S_d$ disagrees with the venue's history for a question, every cell of that question on that day scores the maximum loss. This is fail-closed: an innocent venue revision is charged too. After the venue stops serving the data, the baseline rests on $S_d$ and its timestamps; third-party archiving of $S_d$ is encouraged, and verification on a fixed schedule (H2) is planned so that it happens while history is served. Delisted contracts and questions with no market baseline have none; that status is a mechanical function of venue data up to $\Ts$, known at $\Ts + \delta_S$, and does not depend on the outcome.

\paragraph{P6: Reveal.} For each cell the operator publishes the canonical forecast, the salt, the input manifest and the Merkle inclusion proof. In \emph{clear} mode, which the planned policy uses for most categories, this happens at $\Ts(d) + \delta_S$, after $S_d$ is published, so that no revealed forecast can move the market before the snapshot that scores it; in \emph{embargoed} mode, used for categories the genesis policy names (for example vote shares on polling day), it happens after $\tau_q$ and before the reveal deadline. A cell not revealed by its deadline scores the maximum loss.

\paragraph{Verification.} A verifier checks every signature against the genesis key, recomputes each leaf, checks its inclusion proof and position, checks the count, rebuilds $U_d$ from venue records and checks that $Q_d$ follows from $U_d$, $\beta_d$ and the selection rule, checks the anchors in $\mathcal{A}_d$ (\texttt{openssl ts -verify} for each TSA token, \texttt{ots verify} against a Bitcoin node for the attestation, the log entry, and the witnesses' cosignatures), scans the log for other records under the genesis key, checks timeliness, walks the chain to genesis, recomputes baselines from venue history while it is served, and recomputes every score. The reference verifier is open source; Section~\ref{sec:plan} pre-registers an independent reimplementation.

\paragraph{Hiding.} Salted leaves let a forecast stay hidden until its reveal. Binding follows from collision resistance; hiding does not, and a probability has only $10^4$ possible values, so without the salt a committed forecast could be found by enumeration. We rely on SHA-256 with a 256-bit salt behaving as a random oracle for hiding, for every cell between $\Ts$ and its reveal; \citet{halevi1996commit} give a construction with provable hiding if that assumption is unwanted. A forecast published at $\Ts(d) + \delta_S$ can move the market before $\Ts(d+1)$, which can only make later days' baselines harder to beat.

\section{Scoring}
\label{sec:scoring}

\subsection{The market at the scheduled call time}

For a binary contract the baseline $m_q(\Ts)$ is the windowed median of P5. The window guards against a single trade or quote at a publicly known minute (M17); it does not stop a sustained push, and we report how the headline changes with $w = 1$ (the candle ending at $\Ts$) and $w = 60$. For categorical contracts the per-outcome prices are normalised proportionally to sum to one; because prices that do not sum to one reflect overround and, plausibly, the favourite-longshot bias \citep{snowberg2010}, we report a sensitivity analysis with the overround removed additively instead. Market prices are not calibrated probabilities in general: they need not equal mean beliefs \citep{manski2006}, and aggregated polls can beat them \citep{atanasov2017}. We use the market as a baseline because it is public and priced at a known time \citep{wolfers2004}, and because the best current LLMs roughly match it \citep{yang2025prophet}, not because it is the truth. A secondary baseline recalibrates the market with a logistic mapping $\mathrm{logit}\, m' = a + b\, \mathrm{logit}\, m$, refitted monthly only on questions resolved before the refit date and set to the identity until 100 such questions exist, so that the recalibration is itself forward-only. Because the selection rule uses no price, it cannot aim at longshots; results by quintile of $m_q$ and the recalibrated comparison are still reported next to every headline.

The call time is $\Ts$, after the forecasters' cut-off $\tc$. Any forecaster that copies the market at $\tc$ therefore loses whatever the market learned between $\tc$ and $\Ts$, and a forecaster that assimilates market prices partly measures that drift rather than skill. Two design choices address this. The confirmatory slot \texttt{rw-blind} never sees market prices or news that matches the keyword filter, so its forecast is not designed to be a function of the baseline; Section~\ref{sec:plan} tests whether it behaves as one (the rerun audit of H2). The controls \texttt{copy-tc} and \texttt{copy-ta} are scored on every question, so a reader can see what pure copying earns, and the scores against the market at $\tc$ and at $\Ts$ are always reported together (H5).

\emph{Companion benchmarks.} The companion paper \emph{The Real World Benchmark} scores its entrants against a 60-minute time-weighted midpoint at its own commit times, under its own rules, which allow this protocol to be stricter for the organiser's record. That is a different reference and a different question set; its results are never pooled with Receipts results.

\subsection{Per-question losses}

For a binary question with outcome $y_q \in \{0,1\}$ the Brier loss is $\mathrm{BS}(p,y) = (p-y)^2$ and the log loss is $\mathrm{LS}(p,y) = -\log \tilde p$, where $\tilde p$ is the probability assigned to the realised outcome after clipping to $[\epsilon, 1-\epsilon]$; the clip applies identically to the market, and the share of cells affected by clipping is reported. With clipping the log score is not strictly proper near the bounds, since all reports beyond the clip score alike. Categorical questions use the multi-category Brier score, and ordered categories and numeric questions use the unnormalised ranked probability score (RPS) over the venue's brackets, so that forecast and market share a support. The paired loss difference for cell $(f,q,d)$ is
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_{f,q,d} = L(p_{f,q,d}, y_q) - L(m_q(\Ts(d)), y_q),
\end{equation}
negative when the forecaster beats the market. For the Brier loss, $\Delta = (p-m)(p+m-2y)$. Pairing removes the part of the loss common to both forecasts, but the variance of $\Delta$ still depends on the question, and the mean is dominated by questions where the market is uncertain; it is a weighting choice, which is why results by market-price quintile, and a sensitivity analysis weighting each question by the inverse variance of the market's own Brier loss $m(1-m)(1-2m)^2$ (floored), are reported.

The \emph{maximum loss} charged to an unrevealed, untimely, unanchored, snapshot-mismatched or abstained cell is the largest loss any admissible forecast could have scored for the realised outcome: $1$ for the binary Brier score, $2$ for the multi-category Brier score, $\max(j-1, K-j)$ for the unnormalised RPS over $K$ ordered categories when the outcome is category $j$, and $-\log \epsilon$ for the clipped log loss. Abstention is charged in every slot, so it can never improve a score, and no abstention carries a market price into the market-blind slot. The abstention rate of every slot is reported.

\subsection{Unit of analysis and the headline estimand}

The unit of analysis is the question. The headline cell for $(f,q)$ is the cell from the day $q$ entered the record, whose existence and lead time are fixed at $\tQ$ of that day and do not depend on when or how $q$ resolves. Later-day cells of the same question are scored and reported as a secondary, question-averaged estimate, never pooled with the headline. The headline estimand for slot $f$ over a window $W$ is
\begin{equation}
  \bar\Delta_f = \frac{1}{|W|} \sum_{q \in W} \Delta_{f,q,d_0(q)},
\end{equation}
the equally weighted mean over the window's H6-eligible questions (below) that resolve by the analysis date and are not void, with each question weighted once in every estimator. Brier and log losses are reported separately, and categorical and numeric questions are reported per loss type and never pooled with binary ones. Void questions are listed and excluded for both forecaster and market under the venue's or frozen void rule. We report the raw mean losses of every forecaster and baseline, $n$ per category, and the same quantities by venue, category, region and horizon bucket (1--7, 8--14 and 15--30 days), since the market's relative standing changes with horizon \citep{yang2025prophet}. We do not headline Brier skill-score ratios or Murphy components on small samples, partly because the plug-in decomposition is biased \citep{ferro2012bias}; the minimum sample for reporting them is a genesis constant.

\paragraph{Early determination.} Settlement often lags determination. For Kalshi, a question's determination time is the earlier of the venue's \texttt{occurrence\_datetime}, when present, and \texttt{settlement\_ts} minus \texttt{settlement\_timer\_seconds} \citep{kalshi_market}. A cell is \emph{early-determined} if its question's determination time is before $\Ts(d) + \Gamma$. Such cells are scored in the headline, because dropping them would condition on the outcome: a ``Yes'' can often be determined early, a ``No'' usually only at $\tau_q$. They are counted, and a sensitivity analysis excludes them. This rule governs every claim that cites the Receipts record; the companion \emph{Don't Ask a Model, Ask a World} currently excludes questions whose outcome was public before the cut-off from its own scoring, which is a different estimand.

\paragraph{Windows.} A window is defined by commitment dates, never by resolution. A question is \emph{H6-eligible} if it is a binary Kalshi question with a market baseline at $\Ts(d_0)$; eligibility is known at $\Ts + \delta_S$ and does not depend on the outcome. $W_1$ starts on a date fixed in the genesis record and closes at the end of the first day that is at least 21 days after its start and on which the cumulative number of H6-eligible questions committed in it reaches $N_1$, or at the end of day 90 if that comes first; every question committed on the closing day is included. With at most $k_{\mathrm{new}}$ new questions a day, $W_1$ spans at least three commitment weeks. The analysis date is the close date plus $H_{\max}$ plus 30 days, after which dispute windows have passed for questions that settled on time. A question still unresolved on the analysis date (for example under a long dispute) is excluded from the headline and counted; because the delay may depend on the outcome, we also report the headline with each such question scored under each of its possible outcomes, the extremes giving a bound. A pilot window precedes $W_1$; its dates and its sole use (estimating the variance of $\Delta$ and the clustering design effect, reported as the achieved minimum detectable effect before $W_1$ is analysed) are fixed at genesis, it does not change $N_1$ or the window dates, and it is excluded from $W_1$.

\subsection{Inference under dependence}

Questions in one event share an outcome driver, and questions committed in the same week share news. Clusters are the venue's event identifiers, which the operator does not choose. The primary interval and test for $\bar\Delta_f$ use a wild cluster bootstrap-t with Rademacher weights over those clusters \citep{cameron2008bootstrap}, with 9{,}999 replicates and a seed fixed at genesis, the same procedure as the companion paper \emph{The Real World Benchmark}; the day-block bootstrap of the earlier launch-ledger design is not used for any Receipts claim. If fewer than 30 clusters enter the comparison, H6 is reported as \emph{not tested}, with the descriptive interval only, not as a non-rejection. As sensitivity analyses we report a two-way cluster-robust interval over event and commitment week \citep{cameron2011multiway} and a Diebold--Mariano-type $t$ statistic on the question-level differences with cluster-robust variance, read, following \citet{diebold2015}, as a comparison of forecasts rather than of models. The \citet{giacomini2006} conditional test, with lead time and market-price quintile as question-level conditioning variables, is exploratory, because its framework assumes forecasts from estimators with limited memory, which an LLM pipeline need not be. For numeric questions we report the RPS; we do not apply fair-score corrections for ensembles \citep{ferro2014fair} until the effect of correlated ensemble members is settled.

\paragraph{Power.} With $n$ questions, design effect $D$ and per-question standard deviation $\sigma_\Delta$ of the paired difference, the minimum detectable effect at level $0.05$ (two-sided) and power $0.8$ is approximately $2.8\,\sigma_\Delta \sqrt{D/n}$. With $n = 100$ and $D = 1$, detecting a gap of $0.003$, the size of the Prophet Arena difference \citep{yang2025prophet}, would require $\sigma_\Delta$ below about $0.011$. From $\Delta = (p-m)(p+m-2y)$, a forecast that differs from a market near $0.5$ by three percentage points has $|\Delta| \approx 0.03$ whatever the outcome, since $|p+m-2y| \approx 1$; disagreements of that size alone give $\sigma_\Delta$ near $0.03$, about three times the value needed. $W_1$ will therefore be powered only for large effects, voids and late resolutions make $n$ smaller than $N_1$, and the achieved $n$ and minimum detectable effect are reported with the result. The honest claim available at launch is that the record cannot be backfilled, not that it beats or loses to the market.

Counterfactual forks (``what if turnout falls five points'') have no observable outcome. They are never committed into the scored record, and no accuracy claim from the record applies to them.

\section{What the protocol guarantees}
\label{sec:guarantees}

\paragraph{Operator action model.} We model the operator as acting only through the following inputs after genesis. P1: when to fetch venue records and which response to publish. P2: how many times to run each forecaster and with what randomness, which inputs reach it, and whether to abstain. P3: the salts. P4: when to anchor, which proofs to keep, which records to enter in the log or show to witnesses. P5: which history responses to publish as $S_d$. P6: whether and when to reveal. Outside the phases: resolution of non-market questions, version changes, changes to the rules, when to analyse, and whether to continue the chain. Everything else in the record is a deterministic function of the genesis record, the beacon, the venues' records and the clocks. Table~\ref{tab:choices} disposes of each input. The model is ours; an action outside it is not covered by Proposition~\ref{prop:set}.

\begin{definition}
A cell $(f,q,d)$ is \emph{admissible} if its leaf is in a signed day record $R_d$ that chains to genesis, $R_d$ is timely (P4) with its anchor proofs committed in $R_{d+1}$, and $q$ was eligible on day $d$.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Time order]
Assume (a) $\Hh$ is collision resistant and (b) either at least two of the three named TSAs are honest or the Bitcoin honest-majority condition of P4 holds. For every admissible cell, the revealed forecast was fixed no later than $\Ts(d)$ if the TSA condition holds, and no later than $\Ts(d) + \Gamma$ if only the Bitcoin condition holds. If $q$ is not early-determined on day $d$, the forecast was therefore fixed before its outcome was determined. The baseline $m_q(\Ts(d))$ is a function of venue quotes up to $\Ts(d)$.
\end{proposition}

\noindent\emph{Sketch.} By (a) the revealed forecast is the one committed in $\mathrm{root}_d$, and hence in $\Hh(R_d)$. Timeliness gives two TSA tokens at or before $\Ts(d)$, at least one of them from an honest TSA if two of three are honest, and a Bitcoin bound at or before $\Ts(d) + \Gamma$; by (b) at least one of these is a true upper bound on when $R_d$ existed. Since $\Gamma \le \lambda$ and eligibility requires $\tau_q \ge \Ts(d) + \lambda$, the only cells for which the outcome could be determined before the forecast was fixed are early-determined ones, which are flagged. $\square$

The proposition is about time order only. It says nothing about the content of the information the forecast used, and an outcome that is not yet determined may still be partly predictable, which is what forecasting is. If only the Bitcoin condition holds, a forecast may have used information from up to $\Gamma$ after the call time, which the market at $\Ts$ did not have; for a reader who trusts no TSA, H5 reports every headline against the market at each day's $t_{\mathrm{BTC}}$, which removes that advantage. An official statistic is computed before it is released and an election result may be informally known before it is certified; the resolution source and $\lambda$ are committed per question so that a reader can judge this. The proposition says nothing about the forecaster's training data: a model may have memorised the past, but it cannot have memorised an outcome that was not yet determined.

\begin{proposition}[Fixed scored set]
\label{prop:set}
Assume (a), (b), authentic venue records, and the single-public-chain assumption of Section~\ref{sec:threats} in witnessed or logged mode. Then the set of scored cells in a window, and each cell's loss given the revealed values, are a deterministic function of the genesis record, the beacon values, the published signed day and snapshot records, the revealed values, venue records, prices and settlements, and the logged resolution decisions for non-market questions. Each operator input in the action model above is listed in Table~\ref{tab:choices}, and each is impossible, dominated, detectable or publicly logged, except the choice among stochastic runs, which is detectable only in aggregate.
\end{proposition}

\noindent\emph{Sketch.} $U_d$ is a function of venue records at $\tQ$ (authentic by assumption), and $Q_d$ of $U_d$ and the beacon, so neither the fetch time, the published response nor the salts affect it. $O_d$ and $F$ are fixed by hashes in $R_d$; positional leaves leave exactly one leaf per cell; the call time is a schedule constant; the baseline is computed from $S_d$, and any $S_d$ that differs from venue history is charged; the windows are fixed by commitment dates and frozen rules; clusters, categories and reveal modes are lookups. By the single-chain assumption only one $R_d$ per day is accepted by verifiers. For reveals, anchors and abstentions, the maximum loss is by definition at least the loss of any revealed forecast, so withholding or abstaining is weakly dominated. What remains is the forecast value itself: the protocol fixes which value is scored but not how many candidate values the operator generated before committing one, which Table~\ref{tab:choices} lists as the residual. $\square$

Together the propositions are the sense in which the protocol resists cherry-picking: it does not stop the operator from writing a selection rule that favours easy or unrepresentative questions, but it forces that rule to be fixed before any forecast, applied mechanically to public data, and followed by a full record. The estimand is therefore performance on questions produced by the selection rule, not general skill. Backtests run while designing the rule are listed at genesis so that readers can judge how it was chosen.

\section{Pre-registered evaluation plan}
\label{sec:plan}

No component of the protocol has been run. Each hypothesis below names its metric, its decision rule and the artifact that will produce its result. The selection rule, constants, window dates and this plan will be committed in the genesis record before the first scored forecast and are frozen during $W_1$ (P0). Every pre-registered question is reported, hits and misses alike. H1 to H3 and the rerun audit test the protocol; H6 is the protocol's first use, a test of the product, and is included here because the protocol defines its estimand.

\paragraph{Ownership across papers.} The confirmatory test of the market-blind slot against the market is H6 of this paper, on this paper's question set and window. The companion papers report the same comparison only as estimation (\emph{The Real World Benchmark}, \emph{Don't Ask a Model, Ask a World}) or test a different forecaster version on a restricted question set (\emph{Belief Assimilation}), so no correction across papers is applied to H6; if a companion later makes such a comparison confirmatory on overlapping questions and dates, the family-wise correction will span both.

\paragraph{Multiplicity.} There are two confirmatory families, each controlled at family-wise level $0.05$. H6 is a fixed-sequence procedure: the two-sided Brier test is run first, and the two-sided log-loss test is run at level $0.05$ only if the Brier test rejects, in either direction. The Brier equivalence test of H6 is reported alongside but is a separate, pre-specified claim about practical equality. H4 has a single confirmatory test. All other analyses are descriptive or exploratory and are labelled so.

\paragraph{H1, integrity.} The mutation catalogue and the strategy simulations are part of the ledger test suite (to be added to \nolinkurl{engine/tests/test_ledger.py}, beside the ledger module \nolinkurl{engine/rw/ledger.py}; that file exists but tests only the pre-genesis format, for example that a changed byte, a dropped leaf, an edited previous root, an in-place update or delete, and a token for another digest each fail verification); each is applied to records from a staging chain. Part (ii) is the protocol-level analysis: byte-level mutations cannot exercise attacks such as anchor shopping or parallel chains, so each row of Table~\ref{tab:choices} gets its own scripted adversary. A machine-checked model of the protocol (for example in Tamarin or TLA$^+$) would be stronger and is future work. \pending{artifacts/receipts/integrity/report.json}

\paragraph{H2, independent reimplementation and rerun audit.} The implementer is named, and the hash of the specification version they receive is committed, in the genesis record; they are not employed by the operator, have not read the reference code, and are paid a fixed fee that does not depend on the outcome. The rerun audit addresses M18: if the operator ran \texttt{rw-blind} several times and committed the run nearest the market, the committed forecast would rank unusually close to the market among fresh reruns. Reruns use the committed inputs and beacon-derived seeds; model-provider sampling is not reproducible, so reruns differ, which is what the audit needs. The audit has power only against systematic selection, cannot detect selection on a few cells, and needs the pinned snapshots to remain available. \pending{artifacts/receipts/reimpl/agreement.json}

\paragraph{H3, operations.} If two TSAs fail, cells are untimely and charged; if Bitcoin attestations or the log fail, the same. A third-party outage therefore costs the forecaster, which is the intended fail-closed behaviour. Salt-backup failures are reported here because, in the score, a lost salt is indistinguishable from withholding. \pending{artifacts/receipts/ops/anchoring.csv}

\paragraph{H4, retrieval leakage.} The forward run of \texttt{llm-only} reads the operator's committed feed, and the hashes of the documents it read are in its input manifest, so arm B can replay them exactly. A date-filtered rerun of that same archive could not leak at all, which is why arm C uses an external search service instead. Arms A and B differ only through sampling noise from the five samples and provider non-determinism under a fixed snapshot identifier, which does not guarantee identical weights or serving; $\bar\Delta_B - \bar\Delta_A$ is reported as the size of that noise. Arms B and C differ only in the documents retrieved, so the test isolates retrieval leakage: documents that pass a date filter but carry later information. Index drift and deletions in the external service make arm C worse rather than better and bias the test toward non-rejection. With $n$ questions, design effect $D$ and standard deviation $\sigma_{CB}$ of the per-question difference between arms C and B, the minimum detectable effect at one-sided level $0.05$ and power $0.8$ is about $2.5\,\sigma_{CB}\sqrt{D/n}$, reported with the result. If the provider retires the snapshot first, H4 is reported as not run. A non-rejection is reported as ``no retrieval leakage detected at this sample size'', with the interval. As an exploratory, unconfirmatory companion, we will score \texttt{llm-only} on questions resolved before the model's reported training cut-off and compare with the forward record. That comparison confounds training-data leakage with differences in period, question mix and market regime; reported cut-offs of closed models are self-reported and effective cut-offs can differ \citep{cheng2024dated}; and market prices for those questions come from venue history at the finest permanently retained granularity (three-hour buckets on Polymarket \citep{polymarket_prices}), so their call time is coarser than $\Ts$. Prior measurements of look-ahead bias \citep{lopezlira2025memorization,glasserman2023lookahead} and chronologically consistent models \citep{he2025chrono} are the closest precedents. \pending{artifacts/receipts/contamination-gap/results.json}

\paragraph{H5, call-time decomposition.} Reporting each headline against the market at $t_{\mathrm{BTC}}$ is the conservative reading for a verifier who trusts none of the TSAs. \pending{artifacts/receipts/calltime/results.json}

\paragraph{H6, confirmatory comparison.} H6 is run and reported whatever the abstention rate; abstentions score the maximum loss, and an abstention rate above 5\% is reported as an operational failure, not as grounds to withdraw the test. The equivalence margin $\delta_E = 0.01$ in Brier, about 5\% of the market's mean Brier score in Prophet Arena \citep{yang2025prophet}, is fixed before any data and tested with two one-sided tests \citep{schuirmann1987}, using the 90\% wild cluster bootstrap-t interval. The result is worded by pre-registered outcome: if the two-sided test rejects with $\bar\Delta < 0$, ``\texttt{rw-blind} had lower mean Brier loss than the market at the call time on the $W_1$ questions''; if it rejects with $\bar\Delta > 0$, ``\texttt{rw-blind} had higher mean Brier loss than the market''; if the equivalence test rejects, ``\texttt{rw-blind}'s mean Brier loss was within 0.01 of the market's''; if neither rejects, ``inconclusive: $W_1$ cannot distinguish \texttt{rw-blind} from the market at this sample size'', never ``matches the market''. Every outcome is reported with the interval, $n$, the number of clusters, the achieved minimum detectable effect, the H2 rerun-audit result and the mode (witnessed or logged). The pilot's variance estimate is used only to report the achieved minimum detectable effect. Comparisons of \texttt{rw-blind} with \texttt{llm-only} and of \texttt{rw-aware} with \texttt{copy-ta} use the same estimator and are reported as descriptive; we also report the correlation between each forecaster's probabilities and the market at $\Ts$. Per-question results go to \nolinkurl{artifacts/receipts/window-1/questions.csv}. \pending{artifacts/receipts/window-1/scores.json}

\section{Limitations and failure modes}
\label{sec:limits}

\paragraph{Integrity is not accuracy.} A perfect receipt for a bad forecast is still a bad forecast. The protocol makes a claim checkable; it does not make it true. Early windows will be small and their intervals wide, and every aggregate will say so.

\paragraph{No dedicated witnesses at launch.} Logged mode relies on one public log and on someone scanning it; Rekor is run by a third party, but if it showed different views to different readers, or if nobody scanned it, M11 and M14 would go undetected. The Wayback Machine and Software Heritage captures add weak, independent evidence of what was public when. Recruiting independent witnesses, and gossip between verifiers \citep{chuat2015gossip}, which version~1 does not specify, remain the most important open items for the protocol's credibility.

\paragraph{Hidden best-of-$k$ runs (M18).} A stochastic forecaster whose model calls cannot be reproduced can be run many times before $\Ts$ and the most convenient run committed. The input manifest and beacon seeds narrow this, and the rerun audit can detect it if it is systematic, but nothing prevents it on a single cell. The market-blind pipeline test is also run by the operator. Readers must extend this much trust.

\paragraph{Sybil records (M12).} An operator could announce many chains under different names and promote the best. A publicly announced genesis tied to the organisation's identity is the only mitigation, and readers should discount records whose genesis was announced after good results.

\paragraph{Question selection and markets as inputs.} The estimand is performance on questions the selection rule produces, and the confirmatory test uses Kalshi questions only. A record of liquid US contracts says little about elections in countries without markets. Questions without a market are scored only against naive baselines and support no skill claim, although they may be where a population simulation is most distinctive. A forecaster that assimilates market prices partly measures market drift; this is why the confirmatory slot is market-blind and the copy controls are reported. The keyword filter for market news is incomplete: a paraphrased quote of the odds passes it.

\paragraph{Clock trust.} The TSAs are trusted third parties, and a compromised TSA key casts doubt on every token it signed \citep{rfc3161}. If two of the three are dishonest, the time-order guarantee weakens to $\Ts + \Gamma$, which rests on an honest-majority assumption about Bitcoin timestamps \citep{szalachowski2018timestamps}. Public calendar servers are a point of failure for upgrading attestations \citep{todd2016ots}; a failure delays or prevents attestations, which the protocol charges to the forecaster, but cannot forge them.

\paragraph{Venue records.} The protocol assumes that venues report their own records truthfully and to every caller alike; responses are not signed. A venue that amends contract attributes after $\tQ$ makes some contracts unverifiable, which leaves the operator a small omission channel that H3 counts.

\paragraph{Resolution disputes.} The operator still grades non-market questions. Frozen sources and rules narrow the room for discretion, and a dispute window and a log of every decision make the rest visible, but do not remove it.

\paragraph{Market data.} Prices are not probabilities \citep{manski2006}, and thin markets have wide spreads and can be pushed; the windowed median and floors limit a single trade's effect but not a sustained push. Fine-grained Polymarket history is retained only for a time (one-minute data for at least seven days), and Kalshi's documentation states no retention period for settled-market history \citep{polymarket_prices,kalshi_historical}; once a venue stops serving the data, the baseline rests on the operator's signed snapshot. The fail-closed rule for snapshot mismatches charges innocent venue revisions as well. Whether venue terms allow redistributing prices has not been established; the snapshot records may need to be published as hashes only, which would leave verification to anyone who fetched the same data in time.

\paragraph{Operational loss.} If salts, anchor proofs or the signing key are lost, honest forecasts score the maximum loss. This is intended (fail closed) but makes a storage failure indistinguishable from withholding. Salts and keys must be backed up. Loss of the operator's mirror after publication does not affect verification as long as any copy of the records survives; anyone may mirror them.

\paragraph{Information outside the market.} An operator with genuine private information (for example access to an embargoed statistic) would appear skilful. The protocol checks timing, not provenance.

\section{Ethics statement}
\label{sec:ethics}

\emph{Selection exclusions.} A mechanical rule could select contracts about deaths, injuries, violence against or assassination of persons, or named private individuals. The genesis selection rule excludes those categories, and any contract naming a person who is not a public figure, by a committed list of venue series and categories; the record then contains questions, probabilities, hashes and venue prices, and no personal data. Its risks come from what it lends credibility to. A verifiable record can be mistaken for an accurate one, so every published aggregate carries the number of questions, an interval, and its witnessed or logged mode.

\emph{Trading.} Unrevealed forecasts, and forecasts shown to customers before they appear in the public record, are market-moving information about the very contracts they are scored against; clear-mode reveals follow the snapshot so that publication cannot move the price that scores it. Staff and anyone with pre-publication access will be barred by written policy from trading on the scored venues, and the policy will be published with the genesis record. Outsiders cannot verify compliance with that policy; it is a commitment, not a property of the protocol. The protocol itself never places trades, and scoring against prediction markets does not endorse trading on them.

\emph{Conflict of interest.} The authors are employees of the company that operates the record and builds the forecaster it scores; the company regards a credible public record as commercially valuable. The operator evaluates itself, which is the conflict this protocol exists to constrain but cannot remove. The realistic failure is abandoning the chain after bad results. The genesis record commits a minimum reporting horizon; if the chain stops, the last record will say so, and all unrevealed cells score the maximum loss at their deadlines. The independent implementer of H2 is not an employee and is paid a fixed fee. A record that shows we lose to the market is a result we commit in advance to publish.

\emph{Publication.} Public probabilities on elections may influence behaviour; the reveal policy embargoes vote-share forecasts on polling days, and broader publication policy for simulated populations is the subject of the companion \emph{Governing Public World Simulators}, in preparation. Verification depends on venue data whose redistribution terms are unresolved (Section~\ref{sec:limits}); we will not republish venue data the terms do not allow.

\section{Conclusion}

Self-reported forecasting results are cheap to inflate and expensive to audit. Receipts moves the audit into the record: questions selected from reconstructible venue attributes with a public beacon before any forecast, every forecast of every registered forecaster in a signed, hash-committed grid, a call time fixed by a public schedule that the record must provably precede and appear in a public log by, a manipulation-resistant market baseline a verifier can recompute, and the maximum loss for every cell that cannot be checked or was withheld. The cryptography and the scoring are standard; the contribution is binding them so that each operator action we modelled is removed, charged, logged or made detectable. The guarantees depend on honest clocks, authentic venue records and a monitored public log or witnesses; hidden reruns of a stochastic forecaster and Sybil records remain, and Section~\ref{sec:limits} lists where the guarantees stop. The six hypotheses of Section~\ref{sec:plan} are pending, and their results, including any losses to the market, will be added from committed artifacts.
